## Liberalism as a lens
The following statement was made by one of my professors, teaching a broad class about Chinese history, politics, culture etc. It is paraphrased for clarity and to not fully share the material.

> The more freedom, the less equality. The more equality, the less freedom. [...] Western countries favour more freedom. With the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, the Chinese chose more equality. [...] Communist theory views equality as 'equality of outcome'.

If you've read even a single book written by any communist author, whether that be Marx, Engels, Mao, Lenin, even Stalin (the dreaded boogeyman), it is obvious this is blatantly wrong, both as an explanation of communism but also of China's goals during its revolution. I'm not going to pretend I'm a historian or even that I have a super deep understanding of the material, but on a skim of the Manifesto there are 3 clear contradictions to this statement:

1. Owning your labor value is explicitly viewed *as* freedom. Since it is the largest mark of value humans produce, it is freeing to have control over it.
2. Selling your labor to the owner class, being in "chains" as he poetically puts it towards the end, is a form of servitude; structurally dissimilar to feudalism in its goals, but still servitude regardless.
3. Equality of outcome is impossible, nor desired. There are definitively unequal things that must happen during a revolution: The owner class has to be stripped of its power, and often that is not voluntarily and thus violent. The goal, as I mentioned before, is owning your labor value, and structuring society where it cannot be taken away again.

I won't provide quotes since I'm just sketching ideas from memory, but it is a short text and I don't think I got anything too wrong in this assessment. A capitalist welfare state, such as one that distributes UBI, would be much closer to "equality of outcome", as it does nothing to address class and simply distributes financial essentials evenly.

Freedoms under capitalism are usually the freedom to gain wealth, or monopoly, and if you are a working class member, to sell your labor to those who accrue the wealth. Freedoms under a socialist system certainly are defined, they're just different. Ideas like free speech are not exclusive to one or the other; they are social values placed in parallel or on top of economic ones. Nothing about socialism demands that people shut up and accept their conditions, and nothing about capitalism guarantees it; I think it should be pointed out that third world capitalist countries often employ methods equally or more brutal than socialist ones in order to provide western powers with material benefits and political alliance.

## Liberalism as messaging
This is the last thing I want to touch on, because I see it in conversations here frequently. A big deal has been made of the fact that socialist countries have "bad" or restrictive media industries, i.e. less entertaining, less global influence, less diversity. Ignoring the *many* exceptions, I think there is something to be said about how... *toothless* liberal art is on many levels.

Yes, because we do not restrict speech in really any way, art can say whatever the hell it wants. You can blatantly push fascism, you can say a revolution is in high order, nobody will stop you. However, any *successful* art under capitalism is instantly made a commodity, boiled to its marketable components and resold over and over again until people forget what it was about in the first place.

Some examples:
1. *Squid Game* is a TV show about how South Korean capitalism sucks. Workers are constantly in debt and brutalized if they attempt to improve their material conditions through collective action. As a "solution", many opt into a game show where 99% of contestants are murdered for a chance to win a life-changing sum. Since its release, it has been turned into an *actual* game show of similar stakes (minus the death of course), and is constantly parodied in other media (Youtube series, games etc) with the core messaging removed; only the mechanics of the show are present.
2. *Mirror's Edge* is a video game about a fugitive constantly evading and escaping a fascist police state. She is the daughter of attempted revolutionaries who were murdered by the state, and shows no mercy in her efforts to disarm the state; you frequently kill the fascist police and military, and your actions are viewed by the narrative as positive. It is critically acclaimed but did not sell much by industry standards at the time. Many years later, its sequel *Mirror's Edge: Catalyst* soft resets the story and tells it again in an attempt to gain a larger audience. This time, while the state is still fascist, the attempted revolutionaries are viewed as equally bad for wanting to use violence to force change, and you eventually thwart them. The main character refuses to kill, only disarming the fascist cops who will not make the same concession.

So, two examples that differ in their implementation of Liberalism, but the result is the same: the "bite" of their arguments are slowly removed, and what's left is product, safely reassuring you that everything is fine. There was no attempted suppression here; the original materials are preserved and widely available, but what did it change? Did it inspire new revolutionary art, new policies, new ways of thinking? No, those were the first things to go as success was approached or gained.

Many projects in socialist countries do tend to create a very stifled, narrow lens, but it is not inherent to the system itself, rather it is a reactionary policy designed to prevent this commodification; you inject so much messaging, so much strict, undeniable policy that it is impossible to remove it from its original context. Does it make for good art? Usually not, I'll give the liberals that much.

A more suitable parallel might be the later half of the USSR, once restrictions began to lift. A lot of interesting art came from that time, whether it be paintings, writings, movies or even video games (I think most people know about how Tetris came about at this point). That art reflected the reality of Soviet life just fine. China in the present day still applies a "filter" to many things, but even that cannot stop people from creation in the internet age. It is not only Wolf Warrior films running 24/7 on people's screens, but Liberalism certainly thinks that idea is calming to us, and so it becomes the narrative through reinforcement: "China has no meaningful art as long as it is run by a socialist party, who believes in the opposite of freedom. The solution? Well, more freedom of course. How do we achieve freedom? We won't tell you what to think, but it starts with an L..."
