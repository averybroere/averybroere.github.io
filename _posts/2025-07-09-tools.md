## Tooling and the butterfly effect
I am entering the later stages of a small project. It is basically nothing, honestly I would barely call it a game, moreso just a test of various tools in a monetized wrapping paper; a 3D scene, some assembled UI, and Steamworks integration. If you had any experience in a major engine, whether that be Unity, Unreal etc, then you could probably assemble all of this in less than a day.

![Screenshot](https://averybroere.github.io/assets/mbscreenshot.png)

So, what makes this special then, if anything at all? I decided to not use any major engines. Under the hood, I either contributed to or fully created much of the foundational tooling:
- I've been interested for some time in a solution to protect assets in my projects, since only the most popular engines tend to feature that built-in. As such, any assets are being encrypted and decrypted by [bytepack](https://github.com/ZeroIPDev/bytepack), a system I fully designed myself; I'm not going to claim it is secure by any means, but for client-side asset protection, is anything? It gets the job done and prevents surface-level asset grabbing, plus there is plenty of room to improve it over time.
- The 3D renderer is a fork of [Away3D](https://github.com/ZeroIPDev/away3d), an old-timer originating from the Flash days. I didn't add *too* much to it, but I did slot in support to hold 3D Sprites inside one of its supported map formats, in order to easily place that silly looking tree in the scene.
- On the topic of the map format, I also forked the mapping tool [AwayBuilder](https://github.com/ZeroIPDev/AwayBuilder), and added 3D Sprite support to keep it in tandem with the engine. It was a hacky fix, but it works!
- The Steamworks integration is also being handled by [my own fork](https://github.com/ZeroIPDev/SteamWrap), since the existing Haxe wrapper did not support Steam Inventory, the feature this entire project basically revolves around.

I got to poke around in multiple languages, build on different skills and develop tools I can carry with me into future projects, since *almost* nothing here is hacked-in. An objectively shallow idea created a positive butterfly effect I will reap the benefits of later.

## So should we all be doing this, all the time?
Well, *no*. I am not suggesting every developer abandons UE and Unity and starts doing absolutely everything from scratch, especially because I did not do that anyway. Large software companies or AAA game developers do not want to deal with a stack of rapidly-dated technology, and would rather outsource everything low-level, for good reason: refusing to do that is what placed companies like Bethesda in their current predicament, with a massive staff trained to only use tools that are buckling under their own weight so severely, the completed products *do not work* half the time. Likewise, small developers do not want to take on a ridiculous workload, like doing all their graphics and networking pipelines from scratch.

Rather, if you can afford to (and most hobbyists/small devs can, because our main cost is time), *scaling down* the amount of outsourced tech you use is a great way to improve your fundamentals, and frankly it is also very satisfying, both functionally and aesthetically. We are the scrappy underdogs. We cannot utilize the highest fidelity potential of a UE5, or ship to a massive amount of platforms like Unity, so it's worth asking: do you *need* it?

There is also the moralist argument to not relying so heavily on corporate-run, closed circuit software, of course. It is not as important an argument to make in my opinion, since computer science is an ideological nuclear desert, and most people just want what works, but let's be clear: it is *to your benefit* to create, use or contribute to open-source software that is not strictly for-profit. Doing so keeps you out of the hands of monopolies that can [close you out](https://web.archive.org/web/20210126064632/https://www.forbes.com/sites/barrycollins/2020/12/10/flash-shut-down-will-cripple-millions-of-websites-on-january-12/), threaten you with [exorbitant fees](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/unity-is-killing-its-controversial-runtime-fee), or simply be a [piece of shit to work with](https://www.vg247.com/unreal-engine-5-has-been-a-disappointment).

I'm looking forward to making more tools.
